# Vinay's speaking voice

Use this file when writing lines Vinay will speak. The script should be something he can say without sounding like he is reading. Write for the ear, not the page.

## Source and limits

Built from about 2,100 words of unscripted voice-to-text. Five samples were casual (two requests to an assistant, an account of his day, a movie he loved, how to make chai). The sixth was a 900-word unprompted run through the Baari pitch and product flow. That sixth sample is the closest to his presentation voice, so where it disagrees with the casual ones, trust it.

There is no sample yet of him in front of a live audience or under questioning.

Voice-to-text sometimes mishears him ("plaque check" for "plag check", "Bari" for "Baari"). Those are transcription errors, not patterns. Check product and company names against the team's own material before they go in a script.

## Patterns

### 1. He names the topic before he starts

He opens by saying what he is about to do, then does it.

- "Okay so describing my day from start to end."
- "Okay, so basically I and some of my teammates, we are preparing for a presentation."

Script use. Open each section with one plain sentence that names it. "Okay, so the problem." "Okay, so how it works."

### 2. "Okay" is a checkpoint

He drops "okay" at the end of a thought to mark it done and check the listener is still with him. In casual speech it shows up about once every 20 to 25 words.

- "I had to skip my lunch, even breakfast, okay, so I was hungry a lot."
- "Then I had some rest, okay, since the day was very hectic."

In pitch mode the "okay" count drops sharply, to about once every 150 words, and "right" joins it as a second checkpoint. "You don't know what comes in under 10 minutes, right." "Right, this is whole Baari."

He also uses "okay" to flag a point after making it. "So that the cook or the maid has to not make any kind of payments from her pocket. Okay, this is very important, this is very crucial."

### 3. He says it, then says it again better

His most distinctive habit. The first version is loose. The second is more exact.

- "put up some plag checks, okay, conduct some plag checks"
- "to have food, okay, to have dinner in the mess"
- "not very good, okay, it was not very impressive"
- "give me questions or give me some kind of statements"

He also corrects himself out loud without embarrassment. "Okay sorry, demo underscore vinay underscore skill dot md."

Script use. For the one or two points that matter most in a section, let him land the point twice, loose then exact. Don't do it on every line.

### 4. Short name first, expansion right after

- "an OA, okay, an online assessment, which was for the company Zomato"
- "intern and BTP presentation"

Script use. Introduce a term by the name he would use with friends, then expand it in the same breath for the audience.

### 5. He chains events with "so" and "then"

Long runs joined by "so", "then" and "and", in the order things happened. He anchors with clock times ("from 2 to 6", "around 8:30", "9 to 9:15").

He pivots with "now" and gives the reason before the result.

- "Now since I was busy with the presentation work, and for that I had to skip my lunch."
- "Now, for that script, we are generating a skill file."

Script use. Tell things in order. Put the cause first, then what it led to. Use "so", "then" and "now" as connectors. Never "moreover", "furthermore" or "firstly".

### 6. He closes a thread with "yeah"

"But yeah", "so yeah" and "and yeah" end a topic and move on. He often finishes by naming what he just did.

- "And yeah, those were tasty, and here we are now."
- "Yeah, this was my day."
- "So yeah, this was all about Mirzapur."
- "So that's it, your chai is ready."

"And yeah" also brings in a step he forgot. "And yeah, please remember to filter out all the chai patti."

Script use. End a section with "So yeah, this was all about X." He used that exact shape in two separate samples, and it gives a clean handover point.

### 7. Mild when unimpressed, stacked when excited

He has two registers for judging things.

When he likes something a lot, he puts the verdict first ("Mirzapur was very nice"), then stacks "really" and doubles his intensifiers. Slang comes in here too.

- "I really loved it." "I really appreciate that." "He really impressed me a lot."
- "Very very special, very very different than other villains."
- "His acting skills are insane." "He really cooked up the whole story."

Script use. Save this register for the one thing in the presentation he is proud of. One "really" and one slang word is enough on paper.

When he is unimpressed, he judges mildly and by negation, then reaches for a concrete comparison.

- "The paneer was not very good, okay, it was not very impressive. It was more like a fake paneer or something, you know, rubberized thing."
- "The OA was not very good. It was like very medium. I really didn't like the OA."

Small positives are short and flat. "Those were tasty."

Script use. No marketing hype. He would say "the old way was not very good" and "this works well". Follow a mild verdict with one physical, specific comparison.

### 8. Soft quantifiers and trailing hedges

"Some" is everywhere. "Some printouts", "some rest", "some reels", "some food", "did some stuff". Also "a few", "a bit", "kind of", "some kind of", "or something", "or maybe", "and all", "I believe".

"I guess" is his main opinion hedge. It appeared four times in the movie sample, usually tucked after the claim. "That scene was completely unnecessary, I guess, in the movie."

Script use. Keep "some" and "a few" where the exact number doesn't matter. Give the real number where it does.

### 9. Verb habits

- "Went and" plus a verb. "Went and prepared", "went and gave the presentation", "went and scrolled some reels". When instructing it becomes "go on and add" or "go ahead forward and put".
- He adds "out" the same way. "Speak out", "ask out", "vote out", "plan out", "find out".
- He adds "up" to verbs. "Add up some sugar", "put up the milk", "let it boil up", "showed up the entire culture", "hyped him up", "cooked up the whole story".
- "Basically" right before the verb. "I basically went and prepared", "I basically had an OA".
- When instructing. "What I want you to do is", then "right now", then the ask.

### 10. Fillers

- "Okay so basically" opens a new thought.
- "You know" comes just before a word he is searching for. It is heavy when he explains something abstract, like a plan (6 times in 150 words) or a film's themes (about 9 times in 350 words). It is light when he lists things he did (3 times in 340 words).
- "Basically" softens a statement.
- He sometimes starts a sentence, drops it and restarts. "My, there was a statement that he said."

Lesson for the script. Give him concrete events and objects to talk about and the fillers drop on their own. Abstract lines about themes or vision will make him stall.

### 11. Humour

Dry, quick, a little sarcastic about himself. He throws the line away and moves on.

- "Scrolled some reels and all, some social media, okay, the daily dopamine dose, you know, the short dopamine doses. Very interesting. But yeah."

Script use. At most one dry aside per section. Never set it up as a joke and never pause for a laugh.

### 12. Indian English and campus words

Natural constructions to keep. "Hungry a lot", "very medium", "reels and all", "came back to hostel", "the company Zomato", "over here", "over there", "I believe so", "the most latest", "showed up" for "showed", "out of that statement only".

Also "explain you", "members" for people, "not very lot", "and all the stuff".

Slang he reaches for. Dopamine, hyped, cooked, insane, virality, "not very cool".

Hindi comes in three ways, and his sentences otherwise stay in English.

- Nouns for everyday objects, explained straight away. "Chai patti, which is basically the chai, you know, tea powder you can call it." Also "kirana store" with no explanation.
- The product's own question as a fixed phrase. "Aaj kya banega" appeared three times in the pitch and is never translated.
- One short Hindi clause dropped mid-sentence for a product idea. "One of the person of the household, jiski bhi Baari hai, today would vote."

Script use. Write in English. Keep "aaj kya banega" and "jiski Baari hai" in Hindi. Don't write whole Hindi sentences for him.

Campus vocabulary. OA, BTP, mess, plag check, Nescafe.

Script use. Don't rewrite his phrasing into American or British idiom. Expand campus terms once if the audience is from outside.

### 13. Vocabulary

Plain everyday words. Food, times, places, things he did. Almost no abstractions and no corporate language. The one exception is business talk when he admires a smart move ("I really appreciate his business acumen").

### 14. He picks one keyword and keeps repeating it

He doesn't hunt for synonyms. In the movie sample "virality" came up six times in about 100 words, and "dopamine" four times. "Dopamine" also appeared in the sample about his day, so it is a personal word.

In the Baari pitch the keyword was "context", about twelve times. "Choice overload" was named in the problem and brought back in the solution. "Just to avoid the choice overload, okay, just the thing that we faced problem in before too."

Script use. Choose one name for each idea and repeat it every time. Never rotate synonyms. The repetition is his emphasis. Name the problem in two words early, then say those same two words again when the product solves it.

### 15. Lists with the same opening words

His main way to build weight. He repeats the opening words and swaps the ending, then sums up.

- "Just for the dopamine rush, just for the thrill, just for the experience."
- "Only your mom knows, only the cook knows what's in the fridge."
- "All the inventory, all the ingredients present in the fridge, all the leftover foods, all the tastes that family members prefer, all the health conditions. Everything, each and every context that you have in your mind."
- "What exactly is your taste, what exactly does your son prefer, what exactly does your daughter prefer."
- "We have all the context, we manage all the context."

The casual samples had one of these. The pitch had five. It is what he does when he wants to persuade.

Script use. One list per section, three or four items, each starting with the same two words. Close it with a short sum-up line starting "everything".

### 16. He overstates, takes it back, then gives the real reason

- "Two very really mad behaviored people, not actually mad, but you know, they kind of had a dopamine rush."
- "A special actor over there. I guess he wasn't very special, but what made him special was the virality."
- "Which was not very cool, which was not very good, but it was kind of funny."
- "You can add up a lot of ginger in it. Okay, not very lot, okay, but yeah, a little more."

Script use. This shape fits a slide about competitors or limits. "X is good. Okay, not perfect, but what makes it work is Y."

### 17. He drifts toward what interests him

Asked about the movie, he spent more words on one actor's smart use of a viral moment than on the film. He is most animated when he explains why a move was clever.

Script use. Give him the "why this was the smart choice" parts of the presentation. Keep each section to one point so there is nothing to drift from.

### 18. He asks the listener's question, then answers it

When explaining a process he says the question the listener would have, and answers with "so".

- "Then add up some sugar. Okay, how much sugar? So it depends completely on how many members are drinking."
- "Now how much ginger? It all completely depends on you and your preferences."

In the pitch he stacked several questions to open a new part, then answered them one at a time.

- "Now how do you order the items? How is the payment made? Does the cook or the maid pay the whole amount, or is it paid automatically? What exactly happens? Now to answer these questions I have an explanation. Now how is the payment done? So we have integrated..."

Script use. His best tool for a demo walkthrough. Open each new part with two or three questions the audience would ask, then answer each with "so". Use short practical questions only.

### 19. "Once X is done", then the next step

He closes the previous step before starting the next. It appears in the chai sample and in the account of his day.

- "Then once milk is poured, let it boil a bit." "Then once ginger is added, let it boil through."
- "Once the presentation was done, I went and had some food." "Once the OA was done at 8 p.m."

Script use. Use it for every transition inside a demo. "Once the user is logged in, they see the dashboard."

### 20. Hedged quantities, anchored to his own reference

Measurements come with "maybe", "around" or a range. "Maybe around half a glass", "six to seven teaspoons", "around 250 to 300 ml". He repeats the number a second time to lock it in. "Six to seven teaspoons of chai patti. Six to seven teaspoons of that."

When a measure is unclear he gives his own reference point. "I don't know what kind of glasses you have at home, but I have a 250 ml glass."

Script use. Say each key number twice. Tie it to something the audience already knows.

### 21. He gives the listener the choice, and says what he doesn't know

- "If you want a very strong and spicy chai, then you can add up a lot of ginger. And if you don't like it very spicy, then please avoid ginger."
- "I don't know the English name exactly." "I don't know why, but."

He speaks to "you" and softens instructions with "please". "Please remember to", "please avoid".

He does the same with features. "Maybe a call could be scheduled, although it is not made right now, but we could make this thing like that." He switches from "Baari does" to "we could" when something isn't built. He also leaves settings open with "whatever". "Whatever mode you choose", "whatever you feel right", "whatever it suits to our family".

Script use. He should admit limits plainly. "This part is not made right now, but" is in character. Bluffing is not. Keep built features in present tense and planned ones in "we could", so the audience hears the difference.

### 22. He opens with a moment everyone has lived

"Everyone has this question at home in the evening." "Every meal, your mom asks you, or maybe your cook asks you, aaj kya banega." He talks to "you" and puts the listener inside the scene before he names any product.

He then sets up a small contradiction. "It's a very difficult question, yet a very easy one to answer, but what makes it difficult is choice overload."

Script use. Start in a home, with "you" and one spoken line of dialogue. No statistics in the first twenty seconds.

### 23. Three voices for three jobs

- "You" for the problem. "You don't know what's in the fridge."
- "We" for what the team built. "For this we made an app." "We have integrated."
- "Baari" as the one doing things, with active verbs. "Baari places a call to you." "Baari decides a few dishes." "Baari then processes the payment."

Script use. Keep this split. Never write "the system" or "the platform". The product's name is the subject of the sentence.

### 24. He signposts parts and counts options

- "This is whole Baari. Then this is one part of it. There is another part of it."
- "There are two basically modes. One is the voting round mode. And the second mode is basically your Baari mode."
- "Now once this is done, the next thing is delivery."
- "Let's say we have the Baari mode right now on."

Script use. Say how many things are coming before listing them. Use "let's say" to set up an example.

### 25. Where he tangles

He restarts and repeats words in two places. One is long conditional sentences. "You don't know if, you know, the dish that you just said would preferably be, you know, that would preferably be made for your brother." The other is explaining a mechanism he hasn't simplified yet, like the payment block, where "you know" and "basically" pile up.

Script use. One condition per sentence. Split anything with "if" plus "would" into two sentences. For the payment part, write the plainest possible version. "The family puts some money in. Baari can spend only from that. You set a daily limit."

### 26. His natural pitch order

Unprompted, he went in this order. The home moment, why it is hard, "for this we made an app", what Baari knows, the nightly call, the two modes, two or three options, ordering, payment, delivery. He ended on "this is it" without returning to the opening question.

Script use. Keep this order, since it is how he thinks. Add one closing line that goes back to "aaj kya banega", because his own ending trails off on delivery.

## Rules for writing the script

1. Keep most sentences between 8 and 15 words. Join two or three with "so" or "then" for a run, then follow with a short verdict.
2. First person and active. "I built", "we tested", "we went and asked".
3. Cause before effect.
4. Tell things in the order they happened.
5. Concrete nouns and real numbers over adjectives.
6. Questions. He asks short practical questions and answers them himself at once (pattern 18). He never asks dramatic ones like "what if I told you". Use the first kind, never the second.
7. Filler budget. He adds fillers live, so written fillers double up. Per minute of script, about 140 words, allow at most one "okay" or "right" checkpoint and one "basically". Write zero "you know". Allow one "so yeah" or "but yeah" to close each section. If he asks for a fully natural version, triple these.

## Words he would not say

Leverage, seamless, revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, "I'm thrilled to", "let's dive in", "imagine a world where", moreover, furthermore, in conclusion.

## Example

Built from his own Baari pitch, with the fillers cut to budget.

Generic version.

> Baari leverages AI to seamlessly streamline household meal planning.

Vinay's version.

> Everyone has this question at home. Every meal, your mom asks you, or maybe your cook asks you, aaj kya banega? Now it's a very easy question. But what makes it difficult is choice overload. You don't know what's in the fridge. Only your mom knows. Only the cook knows. So for this we made an app, Baari.
>
> Baari keeps all the context. All the ingredients in the fridge, all the leftovers, all the tastes of each family member. Everything that was in your mom's mind is now with Baari.
>
> Okay, so how does it decide? Every night around 8:30, Baari places a call to you and asks what is in the house. Once Baari has that, it picks two or three dishes. Just two or three, to avoid the choice overload. So yeah, this was all about how Baari decides.
